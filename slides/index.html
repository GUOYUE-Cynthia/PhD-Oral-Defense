Linear systems come with this whole toolbox but only if the system is linear.

Driving question: can we learn a representation in which a nonlinear system becomes linear?
We observe a nonlinear system only through data, and want to predict AND control it. Linear systems give a full analysis toolbox — spectral analysis, forecasting, convex optimal control, stability certificates — but only if the system is linear. Idea: change the representation (observables g(x)) so the dynamics become linear, then inherit all of that. → Koopman / PK-NN.

The problem: data-driven prediction & control

We have only trajectory data from an unknown transition map \( \boldsymbol x_{n+1}=\boldsymbol f(\boldsymbol x_n,\boldsymbol u_n) \) — state \(\boldsymbol x\in X\), parameter / control \(\boldsymbol u\in U\). Two problems are posed on \(\boldsymbol f\):

Forward prediction
Data: \(\{\boldsymbol x_n,\boldsymbol u_n, \boldsymbol x_{n+1}\}\)
Goal: given \(\boldsymbol x_0,\{\boldsymbol u_i\}\), predict \(\{\boldsymbol x_i\}\).
Optimal control
Data: \(\{\boldsymbol x_n,\boldsymbol u_n, \boldsymbol x_{n+1}\}\)
Goal: given \(\boldsymbol x_0\) and a cost, find \(\{\boldsymbol u_n\}\) minimizing it.
Two obstacles for data-driven modelling: non-linearity & high dimensionality.

Koopman: a finite, linear invariant subspace

Track observables \(g(\boldsymbol x)\) instead of the state. The Koopman operator \(\mathcal K\) advances any observable linearly:  \(g(\boldsymbol x_{n+1})=(\mathcal K g)(\boldsymbol x_n)\).

We keep only a chosen dictionary \(\boldsymbol\Psi=(\psi_1,\dots,\psi_m)\) spanning a finite invariant subspace \(S\):  \(\mathcal K\,S\subset S\).

On \(S\), \(\mathcal K\) becomes a finite matrix \(K\) (basis \(\boldsymbol\Psi\)) — the Koopman decomposition: \[ \boldsymbol\Psi(\boldsymbol x_{n+1})=K\,\boldsymbol\Psi(\boldsymbol x_n) \]

finite-dim non-linear → infinite-dim linear; DL handles high dimensions.

Nonlinear state space → flat observable space, where \(\mathcal K\) is linear.

A less trivial example: a nonlinear limit cycle

A limit-cycle system (Stuart–Landau):

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot x&=(1-x^2-y^2)\,x-\omega y\\[1pt] \dot y&=\omega x+(1-x^2-y^2)\,y \end{aligned} \]

With \(r^2=x^2+y^2\), the polar form decouples:

\[ \dot r={\color{#1f6f78}r(1-r^2)},\qquad \dot\theta={\color{#5b4ea8}\omega} \]
The amplitude relaxes nonlinearly, while the phase rotates uniformly.

Koopman coordinates separate decay and rotation

Observables \(\psi_a\) (amplitude), \((\psi_c,\psi_s)\) (phase):

\[ \psi_a=\tfrac{1}{r^2}-1,\ \ \psi_c=\cos\theta,\ \ \psi_s=\sin\theta \]

The linear evolution on the observables:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\dot{\boldsymbol\Psi}=A\boldsymbol\Psi,\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}{\color{#1f6f78}-2}&0&0\\ 0&0&{\color{#5b4ea8}-\omega}\\ 0&{\color{#5b4ea8}\omega}&0\end{pmatrix}\\[5pt] &\boldsymbol\Psi_{n+1}=K\boldsymbol\Psi_n\\ &K=e^{A\Delta t}=\begin{pmatrix}{\color{#1f6f78}e^{-2\Delta t}}&0&0\\ 0&{\color{#5b4ea8}\cos\omega\Delta t}&{\color{#5b4ea8}-\sin\omega\Delta t}\\ 0&{\color{#5b4ea8}\sin\omega\Delta t}&{\color{#5b4ea8}\cos\omega\Delta t}\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]
The lifted states still form a two-dimensional invariant manifold:
  • No increase in intrinsic dimension.
  • Linear evolution in observable space.

Finite-dimensional closure is not generic

Nonlinear pendulum:

\[ \dot\theta=v,\qquad \dot v=-\sin\theta \]

Start from a finite dictionary:

\[ \boldsymbol\Psi=(\theta,\;v,\;\sin\theta,\;\cos\theta)^\top \]

Differentiating keeps producing new observables: the dictionary never closes.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{d}{dt}\sin\theta&={\color{#5b4ea8}v\cos\theta}\\[1pt] \tfrac{d}{dt}\cos\theta&={\color{#5b4ea8}-v\sin\theta} \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{d}{dt}(v\cos\theta)&=-{\color{#5b4ea8}\sin\theta\cos\theta}-{\color{#5b4ea8}v^2\sin\theta}\\[1pt] \tfrac{d}{dt}(v\sin\theta)&=-{\color{#5b4ea8}\sin^2\theta}+{\color{#5b4ea8}v^2\cos\theta} \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \sin\theta,\cos\theta \;&\to\; v\cos\theta,\ v\sin\theta\\[1pt] &\to\; v^2\sin\theta,\ v^2\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta\cos\theta,\ \cdots \end{aligned} \]

The observable hierarchy keeps growing.

The Koopman operator is always linear, but its invariant observable space is generally infinite-dimensional.

Finite-dimensional Koopman models are therefore often approximations, not exact closures.

Motivation · Learning the numerical mechanism

An integrator built for Specific system RK-NN

Classical Solver: one recipe for all f
A worst-case tax: robust to systems you never solve
Specific family: solved many times
Worst-case coefficients are never tuned to it,
you pay that tax on every run
Driving question: can we build (learn) an integrator specialized to the family we actually solve?
You solve one family of ODEs over and over. Classical RK fixes its coefficients once so the order conditions hold for EVERY possible f — worst-case optimal. That robustness is a permanent tax: you pay, every single run, for accuracy on systems you never touch, and no other classical method avoids it (order conditions are "for all f" by construction). The only way to reclaim it on your family is to specialize the integrator to it. → RK-NN. Next slide formalizes worst-case sup over F vs expectation over the family mu.
Learning the numerical mechanism · known dynamics

Personalized ODE integrators RK-NN

Known ODE \(\dot{\boldsymbol y}=\boldsymbol f(\boldsymbol y)\). A numerical integrator \(I\) advances the state one step \(h\):  \(\boldsymbol y_n\to\boldsymbol y_{n+1}\).

One step, using the known \(\boldsymbol f\).

How should we optimize the integrator \(I\)?

Classical: \(I\) tuned for the worst case over a large class \(\mathcal F\):

\[ \sup_{F\in\mathcal F}\|L(I,F)\|=\mathcal O(h^{\alpha}) \]

Ours: \(I\) optimized over the family \(\mu\) we actually solve:

\[ \mathbb E_{\mu}\|L(I,F_{\mu})\|=\mathcal O(h^{\alpha}) \]

⇒ A personalized integrator

Classical RK: coefficients fixed by hand

A Runge–Kutta step \(\hat{\boldsymbol y}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol y_n+h\sum_i b_i\boldsymbol k_i\), \(\ \boldsymbol k_i=\boldsymbol f(\boldsymbol y_n+h\sum_j a_{ij}\boldsymbol k_j)\), with coefficients \((a_{ij},b_i)\), \(c_i=\sum_j a_{ij}\).

Taylor-expand the exact step in the elementary differentials (fixed coefficients):

\[ \boldsymbol y_{n+1}=\boldsymbol y_n+h\,\boldsymbol f+\tfrac{h^2}{2}\,\boldsymbol f'\boldsymbol f+\tfrac{h^3}{6}\big(\boldsymbol f''\!\boldsymbol f\boldsymbol f+\boldsymbol f'\boldsymbol f'\boldsymbol f\big)+\cdots \]

RK expands in the same differentials — match each (for all \(\boldsymbol f\)) \(\Rightarrow\) one condition per term:

term set these two coefficients equal ⇒ order condition on \((a_{ij},b_i)\)
in exact \(\boldsymbol y_{n+1}\) in RK \(\hat{\boldsymbol y}_{n+1}\)
\(h\,\boldsymbol f\)\(1\)\(\sum_i b_i\)\(\sum_i b_i=1\)
\(h^2\,\boldsymbol f'\boldsymbol f\)\(\tfrac12\)\(\sum_i b_i c_i\)\(\sum_i b_i c_i=\tfrac12\)
\(h^3\,\boldsymbol f''\!\boldsymbol f\boldsymbol f\)\(\tfrac16\)\(\tfrac12\sum_i b_i c_i^{2}\)\(\tfrac12\sum_i b_i c_i^{2}=\tfrac16\)
\(h^3\,\boldsymbol f'\boldsymbol f'\boldsymbol f\)\(\tfrac16\)\(\sum_{ij} b_i a_{ij} c_j\)\(\sum_{ij} b_i a_{ij} c_j=\tfrac16\)
Matched through \(h^3\): the step differs only by the truncation error \(\hat{\boldsymbol y}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol y_{n+1}=O(h^4)\).

RK-NN: learn the coefficients \(\boldsymbol\theta\)

The Butcher-tableau coefficients become learnable \(\boldsymbol\theta\) inside an \(m\)-stage RK.

Data: sample the family \(F_\mu\), initial states \(\boldsymbol y_0\), and steps \(h\in(0.01,0.1)\); the target \(\boldsymbol y_1=\varphi_h(\boldsymbol y_0)\) is the accurate one-step solution of the known \(\boldsymbol f\).

Train \(\boldsymbol\theta\) on the loss:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal L&=\frac{\|\boldsymbol y_1-\hat{\boldsymbol y}_1\|^2}{\|\boldsymbol y_1-\hat{\boldsymbol y}_1^{(\mathrm{RK})}\|^2}+\lambda\,\mathcal R\\[4pt] \mathcal R&=\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha}\Big\|\tfrac{d^i}{dh^i}\big|_{0}(\boldsymbol y_1-\hat{\boldsymbol y}_1)\Big\|^2 \end{aligned} \]

\(\mathcal L\): one-step error, scaled by the classical RK error \(\|\boldsymbol y_1-\hat{\boldsymbol y}_1^{(\mathrm{RK})}\|\)
\(\mathcal R\): Taylor regularizer enforcing order \(\alpha\).

RK-NN keeps order 3 yet beats RK3

Two real 2-D nonlinear systems, state \(\boldsymbol y=(u,v)\). A 3-stage RK-NN (\(\alpha=3\)) is trained per family over \(h\in(0.01,0.1)\); outside that range it degrades — specialized by design.

Van der Pol \[ \dot u = v,\qquad \dot v = \mu\,(1-u^{2})\,v - u \] limit cycle; nonlinearity set by \(\mu\).
Brusselator \[ \dot u = A + u^{2}v - (B{+}1)u,\qquad \dot v = B u - u^{2}v \] autocatalytic reaction with constants \(A,B\).

Highlight: “breaking” the order barrier

2-stage scheme, \(k_2{=}f(y{+}h\theta_1 k_1)\): \[ y_{n+1}=y+h(\theta_{c1}k_1+\theta_{c2}k_2) \]

Classical: Taylor-match → order 2: \[ \theta_{c1}+\theta_{c2}=1,\quad 2\,\theta_1\theta_{c2}=1\quad\underset{\text{(Heun)}}{\Rightarrow}\ \theta_1{=}1,\ \theta_{c1}{=}\theta_{c2}{=}\tfrac12 \] Two stages cannot meet both 3rd-order conditions → order barrier.
Ours: for \(f{=}{-}y^2\), both 3rd-order terms are \(\propto y^4\), so they merge:
\[ \begin{aligned} y(t_n{+}h)\ &= y - y^2h + y^3h^2 - {\color{#b5546a}y^4}\,h^3 + O(h^4)\\ \hat y_{n+1}\ &= y - (\theta_{c1}{+}\theta_{c2})y^2h + 2\theta_{c2}\theta_1\,y^3h^2\\ &\quad\, - {\color{#b5546a}\theta_{c2}\theta_1^2\,y^4}\,h^3 + O(h^4) \end{aligned} \]
\(h^3:\ {\color{#b5546a}\theta_{c2}\theta_1^2{=}1}\) (solvable); with \(\theta_{c2}\theta_1{=}\tfrac12\Rightarrow \theta_1{=}2,\ \theta_{c2}{=}\tfrac14,\ \theta_{c1}{=}\tfrac34\).
2-stage RK-NN attains slope-3 error.

Motivation · Learning the continuous description

Cheap forces, but tiny steps ModEqLearn

ethanol
naphthalene
aspirin

ML potentials make the force per step cheap

Molecular motion needs a tiny \(\Delta t\) (fs), so a long run takes lots of steps.

Take a larger \(\Delta t\) to go faster, and per-step error accumulates over the trajectory.
Driving question: can we learn forces accurate at a larger \(\Delta t\)?
Three molecules from rMD17 (ethanol, naphthalene, aspirin). ML potentials (MACE, DeepMD) make each force evaluation cheap, but molecular dynamics needs femtosecond-scale step sizes, so long trajectories require millions of steps — the step count, not the force cost, becomes the bottleneck. Pushing to a larger step to go faster makes the per-step discretization error accumulate along the trajectory (energy/structure drift). So: can we learn forces that stay accurate at larger step sizes, by accounting for the integrator? → ModEqLearn.

Key idea ①: Duality with learning the integrator

Last Project
Given a system, learn the integrator \[ \min_{\Phi}\ \text{err}\big(\Phi_h,\ \varphi_{h,\boldsymbol f_{\text{true}}}\big) \]
Current Project
Given an integrator, learn the equation \[ \min_{\boldsymbol f_\theta}\ \text{err}\big(\Phi_{h,\boldsymbol f_\theta},\ \varphi_{h,\boldsymbol f_{\text{true}}}\big) \]

Key idea ②: modified equations

  • Backward error analysis: a numerical integrator exactly solves a modified differential equation whose field depends on the integrator and the step size \(h\).
\[ \Phi_{h,\boldsymbol f_{true}}(\boldsymbol y)=\varphi_{h,\,\mathcal{M}_{h}(\boldsymbol f_{true})}(\boldsymbol y),\qquad \mathcal{M}_{h}(\boldsymbol f_{true}) = \boldsymbol f_{true} + h\,\boldsymbol f_{true}^{(1)} + h^2\,\boldsymbol f_{true}^{(2)} + \cdots \]
  • Inverse modified equation: Given the true field \(\boldsymbol f_{true}\) we want the discrete map to reproduce, find the field \(\boldsymbol f_\theta\) to feed the integrator so that its numerical flow exactly matches the true flow.
\[ \Phi_{h,\boldsymbol f_{\theta}}(\boldsymbol y) = \varphi_{h,\,\mathcal{M}_{h}(\boldsymbol f_{\theta})}(\boldsymbol y) \iff \varphi_{h,\,\boldsymbol f_{true}}(\boldsymbol y) \approx \Phi_{h, \mathcal{M}_{h}^{-1}(\boldsymbol f_{true})}(\boldsymbol y)\]

The integrator's “true” target \(\boldsymbol{f}_{\theta}\) is \(\mathcal{M}_{h}^{-1}(\boldsymbol f_{true})\), not \(\boldsymbol f_{true}\).

Method: ModEqLearn

Train the modified equation of \(\boldsymbol f_\theta\) matches \(\boldsymbol f_{\text{true}}\):
\[ \min_\theta\ \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol y\sim\mathcal D}\big\|\,\mathcal M_h(\boldsymbol f_\theta)(\boldsymbol y) - \boldsymbol f_{\text{true}}(\boldsymbol y)\,\big\|^2 \]
Data: states \(\boldsymbol y\sim\mathcal D\) sampled over the domain, each paired with the true field \(\boldsymbol f_{\text{true}}(\boldsymbol y)\).
Classical field matching vs. modified-equation-aware training.

Case study: molecular dynamics Hamiltonian · leapfrog

Hamiltonian System positions \(\boldsymbol q\), momenta \(\boldsymbol p\)dynamics from partials of \(H\):

\[ \begin{aligned} H(\boldsymbol q,\boldsymbol p)&=\tfrac12\,\boldsymbol p^\top\!\boldsymbol M^{-1}\boldsymbol p+V(\boldsymbol q)\\[3pt] \dot{\boldsymbol q}&=\phantom{-}\partial_{\boldsymbol p} H=\boldsymbol M^{-1}\boldsymbol p\\[2pt] \dot{\boldsymbol p}&=-\partial_{\boldsymbol q} H=-\nabla V(\boldsymbol q) \end{aligned} \]

Symplectic Leapfrog (Störmer–Verlet)

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol p_{n+\frac12}&=\boldsymbol p_n-\tfrac{h}{2}\,\nabla V(\boldsymbol q_n)\\[1pt] \boldsymbol q_{n+1}&=\boldsymbol q_n+h\,\boldsymbol M^{-1}\boldsymbol p_{n+\frac12}\\[1pt] \boldsymbol p_{n+1}&=\boldsymbol p_{n+\frac12}-\tfrac{h}{2}\,\nabla V(\boldsymbol q_{n+1}) \end{aligned} \]

Molecular ML learns the potential \(V_\theta\), \(H_\theta=T(\boldsymbol p)+V_\theta(\boldsymbol q)\), field \(\boldsymbol f_\theta=(\partial_{\boldsymbol p} H_\theta, -\partial_{\boldsymbol q} H_\theta)=(\boldsymbol M^{-1}\boldsymbol p,\,-\nabla V_\theta)\):

Classical MACE: pointwise fit of \(V_\theta\) to energies & forces: \[ \min_\theta\ \textstyle\sum\big(\|E-V_\theta\|^2+\|\boldsymbol F+\nabla V_\theta\|^2\big) \]
Ours (ModEqLearn): leapfrog's modified Hamiltonian of \(H_\theta\): \[ \tilde H^{(2)}_\theta=T(\boldsymbol p)+V_\theta+\tfrac{h^2}{24}\big(2\,\boldsymbol v^\top\nabla^2 V_\theta\,\boldsymbol v-\|\nabla V_\theta\|^2\big),\quad \boldsymbol v=\boldsymbol M^{-1}\boldsymbol p \] \[ \min_\theta\ \mathbb E\big\|\mathcal M_h(\boldsymbol f_\theta)-\boldsymbol f_{\text{true}}\big\|^2=\mathbb E\big\|\nabla_{(\boldsymbol q,\boldsymbol p)}\tilde H^{(2)}_\theta-\nabla_{(\boldsymbol q,\boldsymbol p)}H_{\text{true}}\big\|^2 \]

A perfect pointwise \(V_\theta\) may drift in leapfrog at large step size   →  the gap our work closes.

Summary

Three works = three components of computing a dynamical system (system to trajectory). Part I / PK-NN learns the representation (Koopman) - keeps linear evolution in the lifted space; Part II / RK-NN learns the integrator - keeps consistency / order; Part III / ModEqLearn learns the equation (integrator-aware) - keeps geometric / symplectic structure. Connection: II and III are dual - you close the same discrete-map vs true-flow gap by tuning the scheme (II) or the equation (III), via modified equations. Limitations: value depends on whether the chosen structure fits the problem - Koopman scaling K(u) to very high dimensions, RK-NN generalization outside training, ModEqLearn still ongoing. Future: long-time molecular simulation, rare-event & conformational dynamics, nonlinear prediction & control - where better computational structure yields better scientific conclusions.
